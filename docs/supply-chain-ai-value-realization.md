# Supply Chain AI Value Realization
### Business plan for a specialist implementation consultancy

---

## 1. The wedge

**Do not compete on implementation. Compete on the aftermath.**

Enterprises have already bought the platforms — Blue Yonder, o9, Kinaxis, SAP IBP, Oracle Fusion SCM. The Big 4 and Capgemini/TCS already own that initial deployment work, and you cannot outbid them for it.

The opening is what happens 12–24 months later:

> 85% of organizations increased AI investment in the past year. Only 6% saw ROI in under a year.

That gap is your entire business. A company spent $4M on a platform, uses maybe 30% of it, and the executive who signed off needs to justify the spend to the board. Nobody's practice is built around fixing that specifically.

**Positioning statement:**
> "We don't sell you a platform. We make the one you already bought pay for itself."

**Why this beats fighting the Big 4 head-on:**

| | Big 4 / GSI | You |
|---|---|---|
| Sells | Multi-year transformation | Fixed-scope remediation |
| Buyer | CIO, CDO | VP Supply Chain, COO |
| Deal size | $2M–20M | $50K–500K |
| Cycle | 9–18 months | 6–12 weeks |
| Incentive | Bill more hours | Prove value fast |

Critically, the buyer is *different*. The CIO who signed the original contract has ego invested in calling it a success. The VP of Supply Chain who has to live with it every day does not. Sell to the second person.

## 2. Service architecture

Productize hard. Open-ended consulting kills small firms — scope creep destroys margin and you can't build repeatable IP.

### Tier 1 — Adoption Audit (the wedge product)
- **Duration:** 3–4 weeks, fixed fee
- **Price:** $35K–60K
- **Deliverable:** Written assessment covering (a) licensed capability vs. actually-used capability, (b) where value is leaking in dollar terms, (c) prioritized remediation roadmap with effort estimates
- **Purpose:** Low-risk entry. Small enough to approve without a procurement cycle. Generates the case study and the Tier 2 pipeline.

### Tier 2 — Remediation Sprint
- **Duration:** 8–12 weeks, fixed scope
- **Price:** $100K–250K
- **Deliverable:** Fix the top 2–3 items from the audit. One outcome, measured against a documented baseline.
- Examples: exception-handling workflow that's still manual; forecast accuracy degraded because nobody retunes the model; planner override rate at 60% because they don't trust the output.

### Tier 3 — Managed Optimization
- **Model:** Monthly retainer, $20K–40K
- **Scope:** Ongoing tuning, adoption monitoring, quarterly business review with hard numbers
- **Purpose:** Recurring revenue. This is what makes the business worth something rather than a job.

**Ratio to aim for by year 2:** 60% of revenue from Tier 3. Services firms that live on project work are perpetually one lost deal from trouble.

## 3. The credibility problem — and the only real solutions

This is the make-or-break. Logistics projects fail on domain gaps long before they fail on execution. A team that has never modeled a freight rate engine or handled EDI (X12, EDIFACT) will get found out in the first meeting. No enterprise hands over their supply chain to an unproven firm.

Four ways in, in order of how fast they work:

**1. Buy the domain, don't fake it.**
Your first hire is not a salesperson. It is one person with 10+ years inside supply chain planning or logistics operations — ideally someone who has *been* the frustrated customer of one of these platforms. They are the credibility. Equity if you can't afford salary.

**2. Vendor partner programs — the fastest legitimate channel.**
o9, Kinaxis, and Blue Yonder all run partner programs. This is underrated: **the vendors have renewal risk on struggling accounts.** Their customer success teams know exactly which customers aren't getting value and are terrified of churn. A partner who specializes in rescuing those accounts is solving *the vendor's* problem, not just the customer's. Get certified, then get introduced.

**3. Publish the teardown.**
Write publicly and specifically about why these implementations fail — not marketing fluff, actual diagnosis with numbers. This is how boutique firms generate inbound in technical fields. It substitutes for a track record because it demonstrates competence directly.

**4. Client zero, cheap or free.**
Someone in your network runs ops at a mid-size 3PL, distributor, or manufacturer. Do the audit at cost in exchange for a named, quotable case study with real numbers. One credible case study is worth more than any pitch deck.

## 4. Unit economics

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Consultant fully loaded cost | $180K–250K/yr |
| Billing rate | $250–400/hr |
| Target utilization | 65–75% |
| Gross margin | 40–55% |
| Revenue to sustain team of 5 | ~$1.8M–2.2M |

**The cash flow trap:** enterprises pay net 60–90. You pay salaries every two weeks. A firm can be profitable on paper and die of a cash gap. Two defenses: 40–50% deposit on every engagement, and a line of credit *before* you need it.

**Break-even math:** roughly 4 audits plus 2 remediation sprints per year covers one senior consultant plus overhead. That's your first-year target — not "sign a Fortune 500."

## 5. First 90 days

**Weeks 1–3 — Pick the lane.**
Choose ONE platform (o9, Kinaxis, or Blue Yonder) and ONE sub-vertical (3PL, cold chain, industrial distribution, retail DC ops). "Supply chain AI" is too broad to be credible. "We fix Blue Yonder deployments for mid-market 3PLs" is a business.

**Weeks 2–6 — Get certified.** On the chosen platform. Non-negotiable, and it's the price of entry to the partner channel.

**Weeks 3–8 — Recruit the domain partner.** Concurrent with certification. Do not start selling without them.

**Weeks 4–10 — Build the audit methodology.** The actual repeatable framework: what you inspect, in what order, how you quantify value leakage. This is your IP. Write it down properly.

**Weeks 6–12 — Land client zero.** Network first. Cost-price. Named case study as the term.

**Weeks 8–12 — Publish two teardown pieces.** Start the inbound engine early; it takes months to compound.

**90-day success = certified, domain partner on board, methodology documented, one client delivering.** Not revenue. Revenue comes from the case study.

## 6. Honest risks

**The domain gap is fatal, not fixable in flight.** If you can't hire real supply chain expertise, don't start. This is the single highest-probability failure mode.

**The 6% ROI number cuts both ways.** Some of that shortfall is fixable underuse — that's your market. But some of it is unrealistic expectations set during the original sale, and some is bad data infrastructure no consultant can remediate in twelve weeks. Qualify hard: if their master data is a disaster, walk away. Taking an unwinnable engagement early will kill your reputation before you have one.

**Big 4 can copy this.** If "value realization" becomes a hot category, Accenture launches a practice with 500 people. Your defense is speed and specificity — own one platform and one sub-vertical deeply enough that you're the obvious call.

**Key person dependency.** If the domain partner leaves in year one, the business stops. Equity, vesting, and a real partnership — not employment.

**It's a services business.** Value scales with headcount, not code. Selling multiples are 1–2x revenue, versus 5–10x for software. Know that going in; if you want the software outcome, this isn't the vehicle.

## 7. What makes this defensible over time

Every audit teaches you what actually breaks. After 20 engagements you know the failure patterns cold — which means faster diagnosis, higher margin, and a benchmark nobody else has: *"Firms your size using this platform typically capture 40% of licensed capability. You're at 22%. Here's the gap in dollars."*

That accumulated pattern library is the real asset. Same logic as the cross-customer data moat discussed earlier — different market, same principle. Start recording it from client zero.
